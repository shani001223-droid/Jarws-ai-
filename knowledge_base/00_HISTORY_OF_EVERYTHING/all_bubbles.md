# Asset Bubbles: Valuation, Feedback, Historical Evidence and Trading Limits

Document ID: `all_bubbles`. Reviewed: **28 October 2026**.
Status: source-checked educational research; runtime integration is pending.
Scope: an expandable analytical catalogue, not a definitive list of every speculative episode. Historical classification is often contested. This chapter distinguishes an expensive asset, a speculative feedback process, an observed collapse and a trade that could actually have been executed.

## What is it?

An asset bubble is commonly investigated as a sustained price expansion that depends importantly on expectations of selling to someone else at a still higher price, rather than on defensible changes in the asset's economic benefits. That operational description is useful, but it is not a directly observable label. A market price is visible; the correct fundamental value is uncertain. Different reasonable assumptions about growth, discount rates, use value or institutional rights can produce different valuations.

An expensive asset is therefore not automatically a bubble. A rapidly growing business can deserve a higher valuation than a mature business. A scarce collectible can provide consumption or status benefits even without a cash dividend. A productive technology can be genuinely valuable while claims on particular businesses are overpriced. The analytical task is to identify which expectations support the price, how those expectations could fail, and what evidence would distinguish competing explanations.

A collapse is also not sufficient proof that every preceding price was irrational. New information can rationally reduce expected future benefits or raise required returns. Conversely, a speculative process can persist for a long time without immediately collapsing. A classification made after a large decline is different from an observable rule that could have identified an actionable opportunity beforehand. HOSHI must preserve that distinction in both its historical knowledge and any future strategy evaluation.

## Why does it form? Demand, credit and feedback

### A plausible story can become a price-feedback process

Speculation often begins around a believable development: a new technology, a financial innovation, perceived scarcity, improved access to credit or an institutional privilege. The story attracts attention. Rising prices can then be interpreted as confirmation that the story is correct. New participants may infer information from the price itself, creating a feedback loop in which demand partly depends on earlier price appreciation.

This mechanism is an analytical hypothesis, not a declaration that every crowd is irrational. Some participants can knowingly pursue momentum, hedge unrelated exposures or value the asset differently. The same observed purchase may have different motives. A candlestick pattern cannot reveal each trader's intention. Evidence about leverage, issuance, turnover, investor composition and funding can help distinguish hypotheses, but absent data must remain absent.

### Credit can amplify both expansion and contraction

When rising prices increase collateral values, financing can become easier for some participants. Additional borrowing can support further purchases. If prices later decline, collateral requirements, refinancing problems or redemptions can reverse the mechanism. The important question is the actual financing structure: maturity, margin rules, collateral, recourse and liquidity. A cash-financed collector and a highly leveraged intermediary do not face the same failure threshold.

A bubble hypothesis should therefore include a balance-sheet map. Who borrows, who lends, what secures the loan, which claims must be repaid soon, and what happens if buyers disappear? The same chart shape can accompany very different propagation risks. A fall in an unleveraged niche asset need not destabilize the banking system, while a smaller percentage decline in widely pledged collateral can have broader consequences.

### Supply, issuance and measurement can distort apparent wealth

Supply matters alongside demand. New securities, token issuance, property construction or newly available inventory can change the quantity of claims competing for buyers. A high valuation can encourage issuance and entry, potentially undermining the scarcity argument that helped support the earlier price. But the size and timing of that response must be measured, not assumed from a textbook mechanism.

Displayed market capitalization is not the amount every holder could collectively realize by selling at the last traded price. A small transaction can mark a much larger outstanding supply. This distinction is especially important in thin markets, where spreads, concentrated ownership and limited active float can make quoted prices unrepresentative of executable size. HOSHI should not turn a displayed valuation into an assertion about available liquidity or realized wealth.

## Valuation sensitivity: an explicit hypothetical model

Suppose an asset is assumed to distribute ten monetary units next year, with those distributions growing indefinitely at three percent per year. Under a simple constant-growth valuation with an eight-percent required return, the modeled value is `10 / (0.08 − 0.03) = 200`. Holding the assumed distribution and growth fixed while raising the required return to ten percent gives approximately `142.86`. These are illustrative calculations, not prices for a real security.

The example shows how sensitive a long-duration claim can be to a small change in the spread between discount rate and growth. It does not prove that either assumption is correct or that an actual decline was caused solely by discounting. A model requiring perpetual growth close to the discount rate can produce a very large valuation from fragile assumptions. If the required return is not greater than the assumed perpetual growth rate, this simple formula is not a valid valuation shortcut.

For businesses, investigate how growth becomes distributable cash after reinvestment, dilution, competition and financing. For commodities, investigate inventories, production response, carrying costs and convenience benefits. For tokens or collectibles, identify the actual rights and economic use instead of importing an equity formula without justification. An instrument's label does not determine the correct valuation model.

## Historical catalogue and source boundaries

**Tulip speculation, 1636–1637:** Goldgar's archival research challenges many familiar stories about widespread ruin and extreme participation. The author's published account and book description support a more careful treatment of the episode [S1]. HOSHI must not reuse a famous moral tale as if it were a complete representative price dataset. A dated historical contract and a modern daily total-return series are different forms of evidence.

**South Sea speculation, 1720:** the Bank of England's institutional history describes the South Sea Company's government-debt role, rising stock prices and subsequent collapse [S2]. Institutional privileges, public finance and speculation interacted. The existence of those mechanisms does not establish that every modern company with government connections is repeating the episode.

**Dot-com expansion and decline, 2000–2002:** the companion crash-observation file records a downloaded NASDAQ Composite window with a 5,048.62 closing peak on 10 March 2000 and a later-window low of 1,114.11 on 9 October 2002, approximately −77.93% [S3]. Those are index observations, not a valuation of each underlying business or a claim that technological progress stopped. They also do not identify a short position that could have survived the entire intervening path.

**Housing and credit before the global financial crisis:** Federal Reserve History connects the housing expansion, mortgage credit and later financial stress, while discussing competing explanations rather than a single simple cause [S4]. The lesson is to examine collateral and funding links. A housing index, a mortgage security and bank equity represent different claims, even when their risks interact.

**Recent valuation concerns:** the Federal Reserve's May 2026 review, with its stated data cutoff, describes elevated valuation measures and other vulnerabilities [S5]. Such a diagnosis is not proof that a named “AI bubble” must burst on a particular date. A measured valuation concern, a predicted catalyst and a subsequent crash are separate claims requiring separate evidence.

## Application zone and the 100% question

**No bubble indicator works 100% as an entry or exit signal.** This chapter is most useful for evaluating assumptions, identifying concentrated risks and designing falsifiable research. It can motivate smaller hypothetical exposure, additional stress tests or closer attention to refinancing and issuance. It does not justify a trade solely because a chart looks similar to a famous historical peak.

An applicable analysis specifies what would change the valuation argument. Examples include actual deterioration in distributable cash, a financing constraint, a new supply response or evidence that expected adoption is not occurring. An analyst must also state what would weaken the bubble hypothesis, such as stronger realized economics or a defensible change in required returns. A theory that interprets both rising and falling prices as confirmation is not sufficiently falsifiable.

The time horizon matters. A long-run valuation concern may be relevant to portfolio risk while providing little information about tomorrow's return. A short-term momentum model can profit during an expansion yet suffer badly at reversal. These models answer different questions. HOSHI should not silently convert a valuation discussion into a near-term directional forecast.

## Failure zones

**Premature shorting:** recognizing fragile assumptions does not identify the peak. A price can rise further, margin requirements can change and a short position can be forced out before the hoped-for decline. Historical analysis that ignores the path, financing and execution constraints can make a correct long-run diagnosis look like an easy trade when it was not. Hoshi's supported paper spot tools do not model all short-selling constraints or authorize real short execution.

**Flexible definitions:** if an analyst calls every rise a bubble only after it collapses, the label has little predictive content. If a valuation model is adjusted after seeing the outcome, it can rationalize either result. Research should specify the features and thresholds before evaluating later returns, including how the rule handles strong growth, low discount rates, sector change and missing data.

**Selective historical evidence:** a dataset of only spectacular failures cannot establish how often similar conditions occurred without a collapse. Include expensive assets that subsequently delivered strong fundamentals, long periods of elevated valuation and apparent breakdowns that reversed. Preserve abandoned tests as well as successful ones. Research on backtest overfitting explains why searching many historical configurations can produce misleading apparent success [S6].

**Data and survivorship errors:** current constituents, revised earnings, delisted assets and retroactively reconstructed prices can distort a study. A chart based on surviving technology companies does not represent the original opportunity set. A collection's last sale can be stale or unrepresentative. A token's circulating supply can differ from total or fully diluted supply. Each measurement must use the correct definition and availability date.

**Narrative certainty:** phrases such as “everyone knows it is a bubble” or “this time is different” are not substitutes for evidence. Consensus can be wrong in either direction. The useful distinction is between an observed fact, an estimated value, a behavioral hypothesis and an unmeasured assumption. Uncertainty should remain visible in the research record even when the final user-facing answer is concise.

## Three real historical examples with dates

### Example 1 — Dutch tulip-price reversal, February 1637

Goldgar's research supports examining the episode through archival evidence and challenges exaggerated popular accounts [S1]. The relevant research lesson is source quality: distinguish a genuine contract, a reported transaction and a later satirical or moralizing story. No complete representative daily price index is supplied by this chapter, so no universal peak-to-trough percentage, duration or fictional trade fill is asserted. A modern comparison must also examine differences in settlement, ownership and market participation. A striking story about a bulb is insufficient evidence for a current cryptocurrency or equity signal.

### Example 2 — South Sea speculation and collapse, 1720

The Bank of England's historical account links the South Sea Company's debt-related role with speculative price appreciation and the later collapse [S2]. An actionable modern hypothesis would require present evidence about cash flows, financing and the particular claim being traded. The case does not establish that government involvement guarantees either a rescue or a profitable short. HOSHI should investigate how the economic rights and funding conditions compare, and should retain the limits of surviving historical price evidence rather than inventing precise modern-style execution statistics.

### Example 3 — NASDAQ Composite window, 10 March 2000–9 October 2002

Actual downloaded closing observations produce the approximately 77.93% decline recorded in the shared crash-data receipt [S3]. A researcher could use that window to stress a frozen strategy's drawdown and execution assumptions. It would be invalid to enter at the selected peak merely because the later data reveal it as a maximum. Likewise, the later decline is not proof that all businesses in the index lacked economic value. Valuation, timing and executable position management need separate assessment, including ordinary periods and counterexamples.

## How to use it to HUNT a trade: a controlled research process

1. **Specify the disputed valuation assumption.** Identify the economic rights, supply, financing and expected benefits of the actual instrument. Write the assumptions that would be necessary to support the current valuation. Do not substitute a famous bubble's name for that analysis.
2. **Collect point-in-time evidence.** Record source dates and what information was available before the decision. Distinguish reported earnings, forecasts, revised figures, supply estimates and actual transactions. Reject an experiment that requires data unavailable at the proposed entry time.
3. **Define both confirmation and disconfirmation.** A financing failure, disappointing realized economics or increased issuance might support a hypothesis. Improved economics or different funding conditions might weaken it. State how the model treats contradictory evidence before seeing the later return.
4. **Separate the catalyst from the diagnosis.** A fragile valuation can persist. A trade hypothesis needs a current, supported observation and a defined holding horizon. A liquidity reference, confirmed structural change or measured financing event must come from real data, not be inferred from the historical story.
5. **Model the path and costs.** Use the next executable decision opportunity, adverse fees and slippage, gaps, limits on simultaneous positions and a bounded loss budget. A paper long-only tool must not pretend to evaluate unrestricted shorting or an unsupported derivative. If the model cannot represent the relevant risk, report the limitation or return `WAIT`.
6. **Evaluate without outcome selection.** Freeze the rule, use chronological test windows, include negative and zero-trade results, and retain all configurations tried. Compare with simple baselines. The fact that a historical asset later collapsed cannot substitute for out-of-sample or forward evidence.
7. **Communicate the measured result.** Distinguish an elevated-valuation flag, a historical-similarity score, a rule-coverage score and a calibrated probability. They are different outputs. A forty-point history contribution does not mean forty percentage points of winning likelihood. Reference entry, stop and target values require supported current observations and remain hypothetical unless actual execution has occurred.

The objective is better judgment about fragile assumptions and risk, not an automatic ability to sell every peak. A deeper knowledge base should help HOSHI notice when an analogy is inappropriate, when an instrument's economic rights differ and when data are missing. It should not increase certainty simply by accumulating more dramatic historical anecdotes.
